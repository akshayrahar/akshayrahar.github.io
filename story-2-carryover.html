The 2-Year Rule: Why Marketing ROI Takes Longer Than You Think

My MSc dissertation at Leeds University Business School analysed 3,163 firm-year observations across the U.S. automobile industry. The finding that surprised me most: the biggest return from marketing investment doesn't arrive this year, or next year , it arrives the year after.

Beta coefficients for marketing intensity at t, t−1, and t−2 lags. The 2-year lag (t−2) is the strongest predictor of gross profit margin.

The Question Behind the Research

Most marketing dashboards are built around one timeframe: now. This quarter's ROAS. This month's CPL. This week's CTR. The implicit assumption is that marketing investment and its return are roughly contemporaneous , you spend in January, you see results in January.

But what if that assumption is wrong? What if the most durable effects of marketing investment , the ones that build brand equity, customer loyalty, and long-term revenue , show up two years later?

That's exactly what my dissertation found.

Model fit across 3,163 firm-year observations (2000-2023). The fixed effects panel regression explained 96% of variance in gross profit margin , one of the strongest fits in the carry-over literature.

What Are Carry-Over Effects?

A carry-over effect means the impact of an investment in one period influences outcomes in future periods , beyond what you'd predict from just this year's activity. In marketing, this happens because:

  • Brand awareness compounds. Advertising exposure today shapes recall and preference 18 months from now when the customer is finally in-market.
  • Customer loyalty is slow to build. A great product experience in year one often doesn't convert into a repeat purchase or referral until year two or three.
  • Search and word-of-mouth are lagged. Organic search rankings and peer recommendations, both fuelled by marketing , take months to materialise.

Reading the Numbers

The chart above shows three beta coefficients for marketing intensity , one for the current year (t), one for the prior year (t−1), and one for two years prior (t−2). Each measures: "For every 1-unit increase in marketing intensity, how much does gross profit margin improve?"

The results are striking. The current-year effect (β = 0.12) is real but modest. One year later (β = 0.19), it grows. But two years after the investment, the effect reaches β = 0.24 , twice the immediate impact.

The same pattern holds for R&D intensity, which follows a nearly identical trajectory (t: 0.08 → t−1: 0.14 → t−2: 0.21).

"Every £1 spent on marketing this year is doing two jobs: driving sales now, and quietly building the foundation for considerably higher margins in 24 months."

Why This Matters for Marketing Decisions

If carry-over effects are real and significant, then the common practice of cutting marketing budgets during short-term revenue pressure is far more damaging than it appears. You're not just losing this quarter's leads , you're losing the compounding effect that was two years in the making.

Consider what the data implies for a firm that cuts marketing by 40% in Year 1 due to a downturn:

  • Year 1 gross margin impact: modest (the current-year β is only 0.12)
  • Year 2 gross margin impact: more significant (the t−1 lag effect kicks in)
  • Year 3 gross margin impact: most severe , the t−2 carry-over effect, the largest, is now applied to zero investment

The firm "recovers" in the short-term income statement but is quietly hollowing out its future competitive position.

The Research Design

The study used a balanced panel dataset of 137 publicly traded U.S. automobile firms across 23 years (2000-2023), sourced from Compustat. Marketing intensity was operationalised as advertising expenditure divided by net sales. Firm performance was measured using the gross profit to sales ratio , a metric strong to accounting differences across firms.

The Hausman specification test confirmed Fixed Effects as the correct model (over Random Effects), controlling for unobserved firm-level heterogeneity that might otherwise inflate the coefficients.
